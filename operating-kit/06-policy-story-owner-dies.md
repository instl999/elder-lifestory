# Policy: when the story owner dies mid-project

中文版：[06-讲述人去世时的处理办法.md](zh/06-讲述人去世时的处理办法.md)

Spec v2 §12.4. **Agreed before the first case, not improvised during one.**

Across eight elderly participants over a year this is likely rather than
possible. In the transition segment (§3.1) — diagnosis, decline, hospice — it
is close to expected. That is not a reason to avoid the segment. It is a
reason to know what we do.

---

## Decided in advance

**1. The family receives everything completed, finished properly, at no
additional charge.**

Not raw files. Not a refund and an apology. We finish what the material can
support, to the standard we would have delivered anyway, and we deliver it.
Whatever is unpaid is written off.

**2. A partial book is offered, framed honestly.**

An unfinished life story is not a failed product. Say so, plainly:

> "We had three conversations. There is a real book in them — it stops where
> it stops, and we would rather give you that than nothing. You can read it
> and tell us if you want it printed."

Do not pad it to look complete. Do not write a closing chapter that supplies
an ending he did not give us. Let it end where it ends. A book that stops
mid-life is truthful about what happened.

**3. Material marked private stays private.**

Death does not release what they chose to withhold. Anything marked
`restricted` remains restricted, permanently, unless they said otherwise while
living.

This will sometimes be hard. A family may ask what he talked about. The answer
is that those conversations were his, and we keep them. Say it kindly and do
not move.

**4. The archive transfers to the inheritor named at project start.**

Which is why `lifestory new --inheritor` exists and why the runbook insists on
it in step one. If no inheritor was named, the sponsor decides — and we record
who decided and when.

**5. The operator makes contact personally.**

A phone call from the person who did the interviews. Never an email, never an
automated message, never a member of staff the family has not met.

Do not attempt to sell anything on that call. Not the printing, not a family
package, not a second project. If they ask, answer. Otherwise it is a
condolence call and nothing else.

---

## What to do, in order

**Within 24 hours**

- [ ] Pause everything. No scheduled messages, no invoices, no confirmation
      queue reminders going out to a grieving family.
- [ ] The operator calls the sponsor or family reviewer.
- [ ] Say only: we are very sorry; there is no hurry on any of this; we will
      call again in a couple of weeks.

**Within two weeks**

- [ ] Call again. Ask what they would like to happen.
- [ ] Offer, without pressure: finish what we have, pause indefinitely, or
      receive the archive and stop.
- [ ] Write down what they chose.

**If they want it finished**

- [ ] Draft from released material only. The §12.2 boundary does not relax.
- [ ] Offer family members the chance to contribute recollections — often this
      becomes the most valuable part, and it gives them something to do.
- [ ] Add a short, plain note near the end saying when the conversations
      stopped. No euphemism, no sentiment.
- [ ] Full QA as normal. Standards do not drop because the circumstances are
      sad.
- [ ] Deliver in person if at all possible.

**If they want it stopped**

- [ ] Export the complete archive (`lifestory export`) and hand it over.
- [ ] Confirm in writing what we hold and what we have deleted.
- [ ] Delete on request, permanently, and confirm when done.

---

## Money

Default: **invoice nothing further.** Refund anything covering work not done.

If the family insists on paying, accept gracefully rather than arguing — but
never chase it, and never let an automated invoice go out. Check that.

---

## Why this is written down

Handled with care, this is the moment that produces the strongest referrals we
will ever receive. A family who were treated well at the worst possible time
tell people, for years.

Handled badly — an invoice arriving the week after a funeral, a chirpy reminder
email, a refusal to hand over recordings — it is unforgivable, and rightly so.

The difference is almost entirely whether we decided what to do before it
happened.
