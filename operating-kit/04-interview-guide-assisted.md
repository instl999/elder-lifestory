# Interview guide — assisted mode

中文版：[04-访谈指南-协助模式.md](zh/04-访谈指南-协助模式.md)

For story owners with mild cognitive impairment or early dementia.
Spec v2 §12.3.

> This is a core scenario, not an edge case. A substantial share of urgent
> customers (§3.1) are urgent *because* a parent is losing their memory. If we
> can only serve articulate, well-organised 78-year-olds, we are not serving
> the market that actually needs us.

---

## What is different

Standard interviewing assumes the person can hold a question in mind, search
their memory, and construct an answer. Cognitive impairment breaks the first
and third of those, not necessarily the second. The memories are often still
there. The retrieval path is what has changed.

So you change the route in, not the destination.

---

## The six rules

**1. Photographs and objects, not open questions.**
Put something in their hands. A photograph, a medal, a recipe card, a piece of
fabric. Sensory objects reach memory when questions do not. "Tell me about your
wedding" may get nothing; the photograph of the wedding may get everything.

**2. Ten to fifteen minutes.**
Shorter than standard. Stop while it is still going well. Ending on a good
moment is what makes them willing to do it again.

**3. Repetition is not an error.**
The fourth telling of the same story is not a failure of the interview. It is
often the story that matters most to them, and the fourth telling frequently
contains a detail the first three did not. Record it every time.

**4. Never correct confabulation.**
If they tell you something that cannot be true — a date that is impossible, a
person who was not there, a conversation with someone long dead — **record it
and move on**. Do not correct, do not query, do not look at the family member
in the room.

Correcting someone with dementia does not restore the fact. It tells them they
are failing, in front of their family, and it ends the session. The archive can
hold it as a claim. The book can hold it as what they said. Your job in the
room is to keep them talking.

**5. Consent at every session.**
Not captured once at the start. Each time you sit down: remind them who you
are, what you are doing, and that they can stop. If they do not want to today,
that is a complete answer.

**6. One named human decides whether this can happen at all.**
Recorded on the consent form. If the person cannot understand what is being
asked of them, a family member's enthusiasm does not substitute. **Decline the
project.** A book made from someone who could not agree to it is not a gift.

## Question shapes that work

Concrete, sensory, closed-to-open:

- "Is this your mother?" → "What was she like?"
- "Do you know where this was taken?" → "What happened that day?"
- "Did you have a bicycle?" → "Where did you ride it?"
- "Who taught you to cook?" → "What did they make?"
- "Was this house cold?" → "How did you keep warm?"

Long-term memory usually outlasts recent memory, so the further back you go,
the more you often get. Childhood and early working life are the richest
territory here, not the last twenty years.

Songs, hymns and food are unusually reliable doors. So is asking them to show
you how something was done with their hands.

---

## Question shapes to avoid

- "Do you remember...?" — makes the question a test they can fail
- "What year was that?" — dating is usually the first thing to go
- "Earlier you said..." — they may not have the earlier statement
- Anything requiring them to hold two ideas at once
- Anything requiring a sequence: "and then what happened, and after that?"

---

## When family are in the room

Often helpful — a familiar voice can unlock things — but brief them first:

- **Do not correct them.** Tell them this explicitly; it is the hardest one.
- **Do not answer for them.** Silence is the person thinking, not failing.
- **Do not prompt with the ending** of a story they have heard a hundred times.

If a family member cannot hold to this, interview without them present. This is
a common and awkward conversation. Have it anyway.

---

## Signs to stop

Stop immediately, without making it a thing:

- Agitation, repeated reaching for a hand, looking for an exit
- Distress at not being able to retrieve something
- Confusion about who you are, mid-session
- Any sign they think they are somewhere else

End warmly and on something easy. "That was lovely, thank you. I'll come back
Thursday." Do not use the last five minutes to get one more answer.

---

## What this does to the book

Expect fewer, shorter, more fragmentary scenes, and more reliance on family
contributions and photographs to supply structure.

**This is not a worse book.** A memoir assembled from fragments, photographs
and a handful of vivid recovered moments can be extraordinary — and for this
family it may be the last record of their parent's own voice.

Set expectations with the sponsor before you start. Say plainly what the book
will be: shorter, more visual, built around what is still reachable. Families
who expect 200 polished pages and receive 40 fragmentary ones will be
disappointed by something they should have been grateful for.

And move fast. This is the segment where the timeline is real.
